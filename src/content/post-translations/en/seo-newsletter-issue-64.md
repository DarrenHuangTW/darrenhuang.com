<h2 id="toc-1">[News] A New Core Algorithm Update + the HCU Ends</h2>

<p>At the end of August, Google launched a new <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-63.html#toc-1">Helpful Content Update</a>. It began rolling out on August 25 and officially finished on September 9. According to <a href="https://twitter.com/aleyda/status/1565620699716374529">reports</a> from the community, most people did not see much traffic volatility, although <a href="https://twitter.com/glenngabe/status/1567131095979724802">a small number of sites</a>—sites with obviously AI-generated or low-quality content—were hit hard. Danny, Google's official spokesperson, said on Twitter that the HCU was not a one-time update. Its signal could be incorporated into other algorithms to keep fighting unhelpful content.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/09/image-7.png" alt="Danny Sullivan explaining the Helpful Content Update on Twitter" class="wp-image-3901" width="396" height="463" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><a href="https://twitter.com/dannysullivan/status/1567243883830202368">Extra information Danny shared while “clarifying” the matter with someone on Twitter</a></figcaption></figure>

<p>Just as the HCU wrapped up, Google announced that a new core algorithm update, called the “September 2022 core update,” began rolling out on September 12. The previous core update was in <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-56-57.html#toc-1">May</a>, when many sites saw dramatic volatility. Because of Danny's comments in the tweet above, some people thought this core update might be when the HCU would really make itself felt.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/09/image-8.png" alt="Google Search Central announcement for the September 2022 core update" class="wp-image-3902" width="388" height="423" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><a href="https://twitter.com/googlesearchc/status/1569346698769571841">The September 2022 core update began rolling out on September 12</a></figcaption></figure>

<p>Neither the HCU nor the core update came with specific optimization advice; only the broader <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2019/08/core-updates" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">official guidance</a> was available for reference.</p>

<h2 id="toc-5">[Research] The Pros and Cons of Infinite Scrolling by NN/g</h2>

<p>Infinite scrolling automatically loads the next page of content when a user nears the bottom of the current page, creating a more seamless experience. I am sure everyone is familiar with the design; even Google's <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-43.html#toc-1">mobile SERPs began using it last year</a>. The approach may look good, but is it right for every kind of content, and does it have other limitations or drawbacks?</p>

<p>NN/g is a well-known UX research organization, and it published this article analyzing infinite scrolling. Although it may seem like a good user experience, infinite scrolling can actually create quite a few problems. For example, users may find it harder to return to a particular product because they will not remember which page it was on. When users go back to the previous page, they are also often returned to the top of the category page.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/09/image-2.png" alt="Diagram of Google's approach to mitigating infinite scrolling drawbacks" class="wp-image-3891" width="771" height="841" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>How Google designed around the drawbacks of infinite scrolling</figcaption></figure>

<p>I definitely recommend reading this article. <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/pagination-and-incremental-page-loading">Infinite scrolling has SEO considerations</a>, but understanding its UX advantages and drawbacks can also help you discuss it more concretely with designers or clients.</p>

<p>Links: <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/infinite-scrolling-tips/">Original article</a> / Other NN/g research: <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-54.html#toc-8">Information architecture</a> / <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-7.html#toc-12">How people read webpages</a></p>

<h2 id="toc-6">[Recommended Reading] Will Getting Pages Indexed Be SEO's Next Challenge?</h2>

<p>The article opens with the observation that <em>more and more SEO professionals are noticing their sites are not fully indexed</em>, then begins exploring the reasons behind the phenomenon.</p>

<p>I really like the author's angle on this issue. From the perspective of Google's business model, crawling the entire web and placing all that content into an index is an enormous undertaking, and the web's growth will only cause its content to expand exponentially. From a business standpoint, however, indexing everything on the internet may be unnecessary because most of it is duplicate content or outright garbage.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/09/image.png" alt="Gary Illyes presentation slide stating that 60 percent of web content is duplicate" class="wp-image-3886" width="381" height="508" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Gary said in a talk earlier that year that 60% of information on the web is duplicate content</figcaption></figure>

<p>Duplicate content should not be limited to obvious duplication such as plagiarism. <strong>Content that merely satisfies the same user need should also be considered duplicate content in a broader sense.</strong> That may be why concepts and initiatives such as the Quality Rater Guidelines, E-A-T, and even the <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-63.html#toc-1">Helpful Content Update</a> released that August had appeared over the preceding years: they could have been laying the groundwork for Google to determine that “your content is not worth indexing.”</p>

<p>A solution for crawling is also available. <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-43.html#toc-2">IndexNow</a>, a new protocol launched by Bing and Yandex, can greatly reduce unnecessary web crawling, making it more sustainable, and Google is also testing its feasibility.</p>

<p>The summary above probably includes quite a bit of my own interpretation, so I recommend reading the original article. Overall, I think the future direction of search engines is something people in the industry should seriously consider. How likely is it that the strategies you are executing now will still work five years from today?</p>

<p>Link: <a href="https://dantaylor.online/blog/is-indexing-about-to-be-the-next-big-challenge-in-seo/">Original article</a></p>

<h2 id="toc-7">[Resource] SEO Content Audit Template</h2>

<p>This content audit template is built in Google Sheets. Columns from I onward contain basic information about each webpage, while columns B through E cover four checks: clicks, impressions, last crawl time, and indexing status. Together, they help identify underperforming articles that need special attention, while columns F and G provide space to record the actions to take.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/09/image-1-1024x417.png" alt="Overview of the SEO content audit template in Google Sheets" class="wp-image-3888" width="1024" height="417" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Overview of the template</figcaption></figure>

<p>The tool mainly uses Screaming Frog, together with GSC and URL Inspection, to retrieve each page's indexing status and GSC data. If you have the paid version of Screaming Frog, you can follow the steps in the article to complete the template. If you do not have the tool, you can collect the necessary data—pages, GSC data, and indexing status—in other ways, then organize it in the format the template requires and apply it.</p>

<p>Once a site reaches a certain volume of articles, it needs a scalable way to evaluate performance. I would not claim this template is the best, but it at least offers a useful way to think about the problem. Consider how you could combine it with other conditions relevant to your own site, such as GA data, article categories, authors, and publication dates, to form a clearer picture of article performance.</p>

<p>This video focuses on the relationship between Google Merchant Center (GMC) and Google Search. For Google, GMC is the more trustworthy data source because crawling webpages and extracting information from them is more cumbersome. Google also recommends that site owners continue using structured data on their webpages to provide product information, even if they have already submitted it through GMC.</p>

<ol><li>Make sure all products can be indexed. In addition to finding them by crawling the website or through an XML sitemap, Googlebot can discover product pages through a GMC feed.</li><li>Make sure product prices are correct. Avoid having Google show the wrong price in search results—for example, displaying the original price when a product is on sale.</li><li>Use GMC's “automatic item update” feature so product changes, including price and availability, can be reflected more quickly.</li><li>Make sure the site is eligible for rich results.</li><li>If you have physical stores, submit a local inventory feed so products can appear in “near me” contexts. A small number of countries—not including Taiwan—could also use Google Pointy to integrate the availability of products in physical stores with online data.</li><li>Make sure “Surfaces Across Google” is enabled in GMC so products also appear in Google Shopping.</li></ol>

<h2 id="toc-9">[Video] Can One Webpage Use Multiple Languages?</h2>

<figure class="embed embed--youtube"><div class="embed__frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/fsXEDDekOFU" title="YouTube video player" loading="lazy" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen=""></iframe></div><figcaption><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fsXEDDekOFU" rel="noopener noreferrer">Watch the original video on YouTube</a></figcaption></figure>

<p>The question in this video is, “Can one webpage use multiple languages?” John answers that, from a search perspective, it is important for Google's systems to identify a webpage's “primary language” clearly. If only a small portion of the page uses another language, that is fine because the system can still determine the primary language.</p>

<p>Google determines a webpage's language on its own. It does not use any meta tag, such as <code>lang</code>, or clues in the URL, such as <code>/zh</code>, to decide a page's primary language. This is also covered in <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/advanced/crawling/managing-multi-regional-sites">Google's multilingual documentation</a>.</p>

<h2 id="toc-10">[Summary] Should I Worry About Crawl Budget?</h2>

<figure class="embed embed--youtube"><div class="embed__frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/LIMqmzuzpVg" title="YouTube video player" loading="lazy" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen=""></iframe></div><figcaption><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LIMqmzuzpVg" rel="noopener noreferrer">Watch the original video on YouTube</a></figcaption></figure>

<p>Crawl budget is a concept surrounded by a common myth in SEO circles. Many people believe Google assigns each website a “crawl budget,” and that when too many pages or overly large webpages exhaust that budget, new pages or content cannot be crawled or indexed promptly.</p>

<p>Gary says that Google originally told people it did not have a concept called crawl budget. Because so many articles and people in the SEO community discussed the subject, however, Google eventually published <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/advanced/crawling/large-site-managing-crawl-budget">an article</a> to clear up the misconception, defining crawl budget as the number of URLs Google is willing—or instructed—to crawl. Crawl budget is still something 90% of websites do not need to worry about specifically.</p>

<p>Lizzi asks an interesting question: <em>Whose budget is crawl budget? Is it the crawl budget Google gives your website, or a pool you can decide how to spend across pages?</em> Gary says it is the former. Based on signals such as content quality and update frequency, Googlebot decides how much budget a site deserves. Site owners cannot directly influence those signals, but they can make their sites easier to crawl through methods such as robots.txt, server capacity, and HTTP/2.</p>

<p>Crawl budget is not limited to HTML resources; it also includes JS, CSS, and other types of resources. Martin says that in GSC's <a href="https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9679690">crawl stats report</a>, you can check whether Googlebot is spending too many resources crawling JavaScript files. In particular, if the JavaScript is unrelated to rendering the webpage's content, the share spent crawling JS should be relatively small.</p>

<p>I strongly recommend listening to this podcast episode. I often see people exaggerate the importance of crawl budget. SEO professionals should not listen only to rumors circulating within the industry; what Googlers say deserves consideration too.</p>
