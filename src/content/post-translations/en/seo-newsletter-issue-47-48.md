<h2 id="toc-4">[News] Current status update and development of IndexNow</h2>
<p>was introduced in Issue 43, which was developed by Bing and Yandex
The<a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-43.html#toc-2">IndexNow</a>open source protocol, according to Bing's latest official article, is currently used by more than 80,000 websites. The change information of these web pages only needs to be sent to one search engine, and it will be shared with all search engines that adopt this protocol.</p>

<h2 id="toc-7">[Trick] Google Sheets formula without plug-in<code>importxml</code></h2>
<p><code>importxml</code>is a very practical built-in formula on Google Sheets. It can be used to capture HTML information on a web page. Readers who have never used it are recommended to enter it in the browser bar.<code>sheet.new</code>, open a file and give it a try!</p>
<blockquote class="embed embed--twitter"><p>Twitter／X Post</p><a href="https://twitter.com/_nitman/status/1477983057940566017" rel="noopener noreferrer">View original post on Twitter／X
The</a></blockquote>
<p>formula requires the input of two parameters. The former is the URL and the latter is xpath (representing the object you want to capture). Here are a few examples:</p>
<ul><li>=IMPORTXML("https://www.example.com","//title")</li><li>=IMPORTXML("https://www.example.com","//meta[@name='description']/@content")</li><li>=IMPORTXML("https://www.example.com","//h1/text()")</li><li>=IMPORTXML("https://www.example.com","//link[@rel='canonical']/@href")</li></ul>

<h2 id="toc-9">[Abstract] Google Search Podcast – The past and future of robots.txt</h2>
<figure class="embed embed--spotify"><iframe allow="autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; fullscreen; picture-in-picture" loading="lazy" src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/episode/48kUCS7XR87VHQkJAixKUn" title="Spotify episode"></iframe><figcaption><a href="https://open.spotify.com/episode/48kUCS7XR87VHQkJAixKUn" rel="noopener noreferrer">View original content on Spotify</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>This episode of the podcast features David Price, who is responsible for legal and compliance matters related to Google search. In this episode, they discuss the origin and future of robots.txt. The rhythm of the discussion is very..."legal", such as what is a crawler? What kind of crawlers should comply with the restrictions of robots.txt? In addition to search engine crawlers, should the testing tools in GSC comply? Or should social media crawlers comply? (As an aside, Google+ does not comply with the robots.txt specification)...and so on.</p>
<p>Gary said something very interesting. Technically speaking, he dislikes the word crawler, because literally crawler seems to mean that Googlebot will follow the links and crawl all the way down. But in fact, Googlebot only fetches the content of a web page, and discovering new links is done by another system.</p>
<p>also mentioned that robots.txt has almost remained unchanged since its inception. Unlike most Internet protocols (such as HTTP upgraded from 0.9 to now 3), in the early years Google actually thought about upgrading its functions so that it could not only limit crawling but also indexing functions. That is to say, you may have heard that there was an early robots.txt<a href="https://searchengineland.com/google-to-stop-supporting-noindex-directive-in-robots-txt-319003">noindex</a>instruction, this proposal mainly allows disallowed web pages to still be indexed by Google, but it was later canceled due to various reasons.</p>
