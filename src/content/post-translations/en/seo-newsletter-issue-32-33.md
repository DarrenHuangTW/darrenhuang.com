<h2 id="toc-9">[Abstract] Google Search Podcast – If speed was a ranking factor</h2>
<p>This episode discusses whether website speed is a profound ranking factor. Some people think it won’t, so they leave it alone; some people think it does, so they optimize every second; some people think it has a significant impact on UX, so they should be strengthened regardless of the ranking.<strong>What about from Google’s perspective? How should speed be positioned?</strong></p>
<figure class="embed embed--spotify"><iframe allow="autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; fullscreen; picture-in-picture" loading="lazy" src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/show/0wJdllV5ZK2FSxguDqQZKm" title="Spotify show"></iframe><figcaption><a href="https://open.spotify.com/show/0wJdllV5ZK2FSxguDqQZKm" rel="noopener noreferrer">View original content on Spotify</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Gary mentioned that changes to Google’s ranking algorithm are divided into two categories:</p>
<p><strong>1. Fix systemic issues (ex: fake news)</strong><br/>For example, if you want to combat fake news and lower the ranking of less authoritative media sources, such an experiment will actually have negative indicators during testing, because these web pages often use exaggerated or even false titles that are more attractive (users want to see it, but the ranking is low).</p>
<p><strong>2. Minor fixes and optimizations (ex: HTTPS)</strong><br/>The other is an optimization with a relatively small impact, such as HTTPS. Now it is just a "tiebraker" indicator. It will only be used to determine the winner when other aspects of performance are the same, because Google does not want to sacrifice quality, relevance... and other more important things because of HTTPS, and this situation happens more often than you think. Google gives each ranking factor an impact score, and then adjusts it upward or downward based on the experimental results to determine its impact on the overall search results. In the case of HTTPS, they revised its impact score down 4 to 5 times.</p>
<p>Martin then said that in addition to ranking, speed will also have (1.)<a class="rank-math-link" href="./how-search-engines-crawl.html">crawling</a>, the impact of indexing, the speed of the website will affect the latter two, which is the so-called<a class="rank-math-link" href="./seo-newsletter-issue-12.html#toc-10">crawl budget</a>; (2.) In addition, what is "speed"? The website loads quickly but there is no way to interact, which is not a good experience, that is
The origin of<a class="rank-math-link" href="./core-web-vitals-lcp-fid-cls.html">Core Web Vitals</a>; and (3.) How to measure speed? Is it based on the speed seen by the search engine or the user's experience speed?; and finally (4.) Is it possible that the speed indicator can be manipulated to make an automatic robot visit the opponent's website at 2G speed?</p>
<p>The above discussion clearly shows that the speed indicator is clearly good or bad in terms of ranking positioning. Finally, John raised another question. Based on the above reasons, how should they communicate with the SEO community, how should they speak correctly, how can they be of substantial help, and how can they avoid being attacked by spammers?</p>
<p>This episode largely reflects their usual difficulties in external communication. Although the topic feels serious, the three of them are actually quite relaxed and happy when speaking. <h2 id="toc-10">[Abstract] Google Search Podcast – Search word processing and understanding</h2>
<p>Gary talked about it in previous episodes.<a class="rank-math-link" href="./seo-newsletter-issue-27.html#toc-8">indexing</a>and<a class="rank-math-link" href="./seo-newsletter-issue-30.html#toc-10">serving</a>, this episode is going to discuss another part of serving (ranking as most people know it): query parsing and query understanding.</p>
<p><strong>Search word processing (Query Parsing)</strong><br/>First, Google must first convert the words (query) entered by the user into something that the indexing system can understand. This thing is called a "parsed query". The concept sounds simple, but it is actually very complicated. The simple part is to identify synonyms and determine the entity; but in other languages, this process will become very complicated. For example, there are no spaces between each "word" in Chinese, while Thai has almost no punctuation between sentences. For this type of language, there is a "Segmentor" in the system that specifically divides the query entered by the user into multiple "words".</p>
<p><strong>Search word understanding (Query Understanding)</strong><br/>The next step is to understand the meaning of each word and expand it into more synonyms (synonyms), but at the same time avoid the emergence of non-synonyms (anti-synonyms). Another important point is stop words, such as a, the, of, and this in English. Most of the time these words have no meaning, but in some cases, such as when searching for "The Lord of the Rings", they are indispensable.</p>
<p>Query Understanding also has many details, such as understanding the user's search intent. A person who searches for "The Rock" in the United States is looking for Dwayne "The Rock" Johnson, but a person who searches for "The Rock" elsewhere may be at a certain attraction.</p>
<p>There is another thing called diacritical expansion. For example, John M<strong><span>ue</span></strong>ller's name can also be written as John M<strong><span>ü</span></strong>ller, in some languages, a word can be written in multiple ways, and their variations are basically treated as synonyms.</p>
<p>The last and most important point is that in the understanding stage, each synonym will be added with a score (weight), depending on how confident Google is that the synonym is similar to the entered word, so the results you see when searching for a certain word and searching for its synonyms will not be exactly the same.</p>
