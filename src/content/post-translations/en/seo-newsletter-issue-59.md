<h2 id="toc-1">[Official] Google Documentation Update—Googlebot Crawls Only the First 15 MB</h2>

<p>Google recently updated several documents. One new detail was that <strong>Googlebot crawls and indexes only the first 15 MB of an HTML file</strong>, ignoring anything beyond that point. The addition sparked a lot of discussion, but the headline may have made it sound more dramatic than it was. In practice, an HTML file larger than 15 MB is extremely rare.</p>

<p>According to <a href="https://twitter.com/paulcalvano/status/1541402096897069056">HTTP Archive data</a>, among eight million websites, only 151 homepages were larger than 15 MB uncompressed, while the median webpage was only about...30 KB. Besides, if the HTML alone exceeds 15 MB, SEO is probably not the only issue—the site's entire design framework likely deserves a thorough review.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/06/image-13.png" alt="Browser developer tools showing a webpage with 118 megabytes of HTML" class="wp-image-3688" width="330" height="402" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>A webpage whose HTML alone is 118 MB (<a href="https://twitter.com/paulcalvano/status/1541402101825298436">source</a>)</figcaption></figure>

<p>The update drew so much attention—Gary even <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2022/06/googlebot-15mb">published an article</a> in response—mainly because many people mistakenly thought the 15 MB included all webpage subresources, such as CSS, JavaScript, and images. In fact, the limit applied only to the HTML text itself.</p>

<h2 id="toc-3">[Knowledge] How Algorithms Rank Webpages, by Search Engine Neeva</h2>

<p>Neeva is an ad-free, privacy-focused search engine whose business model relies on paid memberships offering advanced features. One of its founders is a former Google Ads executive. The company recently shared some information about search engine rankings on Twitter. I found it quite interesting as an accessible introduction to a few concepts behind ranking algorithms.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/06/image-10-1024x652.png" alt="Example of a Neeva search results page" class="wp-image-3680" width="512" height="326" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>What Neeva's search results looked like</figcaption></figure>

<p>First, the best-known method for ranking documents by their relevance to a query is <a href="https://medium.com/datamixcontent-lab/%E6%96%87%E6%9C%AC%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90%E5%85%A5%E9%96%80-%E6%A6%82%E5%BF%B5%E7%AF%87-%E7%B5%A6%E6%88%91%E4%B8%80%E6%AE%B5%E8%A9%B1-%E6%88%91%E5%91%8A%E8%A8%B4%E4%BD%A0%E9%87%8D%E9%BB%9E%E5%9C%A8%E5%93%AA-%E5%B0%8D%E6%96%87%E6%9C%AC%E9%87%8D%E9%BB%9E%E5%AD%97%E8%A9%9E%E5%8A%A0%E6%AC%8A%E7%9A%84tf-idf%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95-f6a2790b4991">TF-IDF</a>. Put simply, the more often a word appears in <strong>one document</strong>, the more important it is. But when that word appears in <strong>every document</strong>—for example, words like “I,” “you,” “he,” and “the”—the less important it becomes.</p>

<p>This creates a problem. A very long document may cover many different topics, leaving it with a low TF-IDF score for every word, but that does not mean its content on a particular subject is poor. A document length normalization step is therefore usually applied so that long documents are treated fairly too.</p>

<p>Even that does not solve every problem. For example, when someone searches for <code>neeva</code>, should <code>neeva.com</code> or <code>neevaneevaneeva.com</code> rank higher? Even after processing, their relevance is the same. Information beyond the text is needed to make that decision. <strong>An important principle of ranking algorithms is that how others see you matters more than how you see yourself.</strong></p>

<p>Information beyond the text includes the backlinks everyone knows about, but query-click data is also very important. Neeva records the query and webpage URL associated with every click on its search engine and uses that data as one of its ranking signals.</p>

<h2 id="toc-5">[Practical] Run Screaming Frog in the Cloud and Control It Remotely</h2>

<p>I am pretty surprised to see Screaming Frog publish its own tutorial on running the crawler in Google Cloud. Running it in the cloud means multiple people can use it with a single license key. Another advantage is that crawling a large website will not consume so many resources on your own computer.</p>

<p>Running it in Google Cloud is not free. The cost depends on how much you use it and the performance of your virtual machine, but crawling 3.1 million URLs over two days costs only about €20. New users also receive US$300 in credit.</p>

<p>One of my coworkers tested the process firsthand. Although it sounded complicated—setting up Google Cloud, creating a virtual machine, remotely controlling that virtual machine, and so on—he said he had it working within about one to two hours. I also helped him test the remote-control part, and we got that working within about 15 minutes.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/06/%E5%9C%A8%E9%9B%B2%E7%AB%AF%E4%B8%8A%E8%B7%91screaming-frog-1024x550.png" alt="Remote desktop session running Screaming Frog on a cloud virtual machine" class="wp-image-3683" width="512" height="275" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Testing remote access to a virtual machine running Screaming Frog for a coworker</figcaption></figure>

<p>This approach can save quite a bit of money by letting multiple people share an account, but it also has drawbacks. Remotely operating a cloud machine is often laggy, and moving the results from the VM back to a local machine adds a few steps. Still, if you have a use for it, I recommend giving it a try!</p>

<p>Link: <a href="https://www.screamingfrog.co.uk/seo-spider-cloud/">Original Screaming Frog article</a></p>

<h2 id="toc-7">[Technical] Chrome v103 Officially Supports Early Hints (Status Code 103) </h2>

<p>When I covered Early Hints (status code 103) in <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-41.html#toc-4">Issue 41</a>, this new protocol led by Cloudflare was undergoing implementation testing. A few months later, Cloudflare published <a href="https://blog.cloudflare.com/early-hints-performance/">an article</a> explaining that the technology can improve website performance by as much as about 30%.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-full is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/06/image-15.png" alt="Network timeline comparing page loading with and without HTTP 103 Early Hints" class="wp-image-3691" width="443" height="508" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Before-and-after comparison of Early Hints (<a href="https://twitter.com/colinbendell/status/1539322190541295616">source</a>)</figcaption></figure>

<p>When a browser sends a request to a server, the server typically needs some time—server think time—to prepare before sending a response back. With Early Hints, the server effectively says as soon as it receives the request, <em>“I need some time to prepare the webpage you requested, but you will need this CSS file and this JavaScript file later, so go fetch them first!”</em></p>

<p>Google recently <a href="https://developer.chrome.com/blog/early-hints/">announced</a> that Chrome would support the technology beginning with version 103. Shopify also helped test it and saw good results. If it interests you, send it to your engineers for discussion. If you use Cloudflare's CDN, even the free plan lets you <a href="https://developers.cloudflare.com/cache/about/early-hints/#enabling-early-hints">enable the feature with one click</a>.</p>

<h2>[Video] How Google Rolls Out Algorithm Updates</h2>

<p>The question in the video is, <em>“How does Google roll out algorithm updates? Does it always begin with English-speaking countries and then gradually release them in other countries?”</em></p>

<p>John answers that Google usually wants an algorithm to work across all countries and languages, rather than changing it for individual markets, because that makes it easier to manage and test. In other words, when an update launches, Google tests the most relevant language where necessary. That language might be English, but not always, and the update then gradually expands into other language markets.</p>

<h2 id="toc-10">[Summary] Podcast—User Experience (UX) and SEO</h2>

<figure class="embed embed--youtube"><div class="embed__frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/HicvsqkgFi8" title="YouTube video player" loading="lazy" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen=""></iframe></div><figcaption><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HicvsqkgFi8" rel="noopener noreferrer">Watch the original video on YouTube</a></figcaption></figure>

<p>In this episode, Martin, Gary, and Lizzi discuss user experience. Lizzi is a lead editor and writer for Google Search. She says that before recording the episode, she looked back and found that the official documentation barely mentioned UX, only Page Experience (PX). She thought the term may have been created from a corporate marketing perspective and agreed that PX did not cover the entire scope of UX.</p>

<p>Google's search best-practice documents did include principles such as <em>“write for your readers.”</em> Gary jumps in to say that when a concept is difficult to define, Google's documentation tends to use vague language. For example, <em>“write for your readers”</em> might mean <em>“write for your readers' reading level,”</em> but Google would not use the latter phrasing because reading level is difficult to define precisely. Within UX, however, how easy an article is to read still deserves consideration.</p>

<p>Other examples include “write high-quality descriptive headings” and “site architecture”; Google could not provide a single standard.</p>

<p>Partway through the episode, they discuss content hidden inside tabs or collapsible modules. Martin asks whether, as Google had said before, content not immediately visible to users was less important than directly visible content. Gary answers that Google did use such a mechanism in the past, but no longer did. His answer surprises Martin, and even <a href="https://www.seroundtable.com/google-not-visible-content-33630.html">Search Engine Roundtable</a> published an article specifically to reiterate the point.</p>
