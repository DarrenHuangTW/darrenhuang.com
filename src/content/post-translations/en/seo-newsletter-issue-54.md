<h2 id="toc-3">[Tool] “Retail Search” for Better On-Site Ecommerce Search </h2>

<p>First, this is not a free tool. It is a new service within Google Cloud designed to make on-site search results more relevant for ecommerce websites. For example, a user can search for “<em>long black dress with short sleeves and comfortable fit</em>” and find matching products.</p>

<p>The product's <a href="https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/retail/retail-search-product-discovery-increased-conversion-shopping-ecommerce-global-launch">launch announcement</a> says that 94% of U.S. consumers abandon a shopping session because the product search results are irrelevant. On-site search on an ordinary ecommerce website is relatively weak and can usually perform only simple keyword matching. Users cannot search in natural language, so this tool's technology can address that conversion pain point.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/04/%E7%AB%99%E5%85%A7%E6%90%9C%E5%B0%8B%E5%B0%8D%E6%96%BC%E9%9B%BB%E5%95%86%E7%9A%84%E5%BD%B1%E9%9F%BF-1024x576.jpg" alt="Chart showing the effect of relevant on-site search on ecommerce sessions" class="wp-image-3510" width="546" height="307" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>The impact of on-site search on ecommerce</figcaption></figure>

<p>One of the questions I was asked while interviewing for an SEO role at Amazon a few years earlier was <a href="./amazon-seo-product-manager-interview.html#:~:text=2.-,%E6%98%AF%E5%90%A6%E8%A9%B2%E5%8A%A0%E5%BC%B7%E7%94%A2%E5%93%81%E6%90%9C%E5%B0%8B%E9%A0%81%E7%9A%84%E7%AF%A9%E9%81%B8%E6%A8%99%E6%BA%96,-Amazon%E7%9A%84%E7%94%A2%E5%93%81">related to on-site search</a>. SEO focuses on organic traffic from search engines, but once the traffic arrives, CRO—conversion rate optimization—is another part of the process that cannot be ignored.</p>

<h2 id="toc-7">[News] Major Publisher Sees No Traffic Loss After Turning Off AMP </h2>

<p>Future plc is a major British publisher whose <a href="https://www.futureplc.com/our-brands/">brands</a> include familiar magazines such as PC Gamer, Marie Claire, and TechRadar.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img src="./wp-content/uploads/2022/04/future-plc-%E6%97%97%E4%B8%8B%E9%9B%9C%E8%AA%8C-1024x578.png" alt="Logos of publishing brands owned by Future plc" class="wp-image-3506" width="583" height="329" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>An overview of Future's brands (<a href="https://www.mediabistro.com/companies/future-plc/">source</a>)</figcaption></figure>

<p>After testing, Future decided to turn off AMP across its websites and use ordinary mobile webpages. Across all its media properties, 80%–90% of pages are no longer using AMP, with no traffic loss. <strong>More importantly, ordinary webpages are not subject to AMP's restrictions, so Future can sell more ad placements and generate more advertising revenue.</strong></p>

<p>Future originally adopted AMP for the same reason everyone else did: placement in Top Stories on the SERP. But after <a href="./seo-newsletter-issue-32-33.html#toc-8">Google announced at I/O 2021</a> that AMP would no longer be required to appear there, the company began testing how to turn it off. Beyond that, <strong>Future also carried out a project during this period to optimize <a href="./core-web-vitals-lcp-fid-cls.html">Core Web Vitals</a>, allowing its sites to retain a good user experience after removing AMP.</strong> Many competing publishers do not have good mobile websites, which means they still cannot cut AMP so easily.</p>

<h2 id="toc-9">[Video] Why Isn't My JavaScript Webpage Cached by Google?</h2>

<p>In search results, you may occasionally see a webpage that Google has <a href="https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/1687222">cached as a backup</a>. When a website relies on JavaScript to display its content, the cached copy will usually show either a completely blank page or omit the sections that require JavaScript rendering.</p>

<p>In this video, John explains why this happens: the <a href="https://medium.com/%E7%A8%8B%E5%BC%8F%E7%8C%BF%E5%90%83%E9%A6%99%E8%95%89/same-origin-policy-%E5%90%8C%E6%BA%90%E6%94%BF%E7%AD%96-%E4%B8%80%E5%88%87%E5%AE%89%E5%85%A8%E7%9A%84%E5%9F%BA%E7%A4%8E-36432565a226">same-origin policy</a>. He also says the best way to check whether a webpage's content has been indexed is through GSC, not the cached copy.</p>

<h2 id="toc-10">[Summary] Google Search Podcast—Structured Data</h2>

<p>This episode brings in <a href="https://twitter.com/rrlevering">Ryan Levering</a> to discuss structured data. Ryan is an engineer responsible for structured data at Google. SEO professionals generally treat “structured data” as synonymous with schema markup, but taken literally, it is not a very precise term. Whether data is “structured” is relative; even HTML can be viewed as textual data with structure added to it.</p>

<p>Ryan also says many people do not understand the relationship between Google and <a href="https://schema.org/">Schema.org</a>. Schema.org is an organization built through collaboration among many companies, and Google is only one member. Schema.org includes <a href="https://schema.org/docs/full.html">all kinds of “Types” and “Properties”</a>, but Google provides special appearances or features for only <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/advanced/structured-data/search-gallery">some of them</a>.</p>

<p>Does adding schema that does not produce a special rich result help a webpage? Ryan says he would not discourage people from doing it as long as the schema was reasonable, because (1) it would help Google see that more people were beginning to use that schema, and (2) it might help Google understand the webpage's content a little better.</p>
